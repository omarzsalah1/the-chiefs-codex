# Chapter 18: On the Dignity of Absence

On the Voluntary Departure

*Being an account of endings and their recognition, and why the wise gatekeeper leaves before he is asked*

*1490*

I. The Recognition

The sign came during a council meeting on the salt tax.

It was spring, 1490. Duke Guidobaldo was eighteen now, tall and composed, no longer the fragile boy Bertoldo had shielded through the succession. The regency had ended two years ago. The young Duke ruled in his own right, and Urbino was prosperous.

Bertoldo sat at his customary position—to the Duke's right, slightly behind, where he could see every face at the table but remained outside the direct circle of authority. It was a position he'd perfected over three decades. Close enough to advise. Far enough to be invisible.

The Treasurer was arguing for an increase in the salt levy. The Captain of the Guard was arguing against it, citing unrest in the hill towns. It was the kind of debate Bertoldo had navigated a hundred times.

He watched Guidobaldo, waiting for the familiar moment—the pause, the slight turn of the head, the glance that said *What do you think?* That glance had been Bertoldo's signal for seven years. It meant: guide me. Tell me which way to lean.

The pause came.

Guidobaldo's eyes flicked toward the maps on the table, then toward the Treasurer's calculations. His brow furrowed slightly. The room held its breath.

But the Duke didn't look at Bertoldo.

Instead, Guidobaldo spoke: "The Captain is correct. The hill towns are restive. An increased levy now would inflame tensions we've worked three years to calm. However, the Treasurer's concern about revenue is also valid. Therefore—"

He outlined a compromise: a temporary surcharge on merchant salt rather than domestic supply, exempting the hill towns entirely while taxing the profitable coastal trade.

It was elegant. It was correct.

It was exactly what Bertoldo would have suggested.

But Guidobaldo had reached the solution himself.

The council nodded, accepting the wisdom. The Treasurer made notes. The Captain relaxed. The decision was made.

And Bertoldo sat in his chair, feeling something crack inside his chest.

The Duke had not looked at him once.

After the council adjourned, Guidobaldo stopped to discuss the salt merchants with the Treasurer. Bertoldo gathered his papers slowly, waiting. Surely the Duke would want to review the decision privately, to confirm he'd chosen correctly.

But Guidobaldo simply walked out with the Treasurer, their voices fading down the corridor. Discussing tariff rates. Making decisions.

Without Bertoldo.

That evening, Bertoldo stood at his window and watched the courtyard below. Servants crossed back and forth, carrying torches against the gathering dark. Guards changed shifts. Life continued, as it always had.

But something had shifted.

He'd spent seven years teaching Guidobaldo how to think. How to weigh competing interests. How to read a room. How to make decisions that served the realm rather than faction.

The teaching had worked.

The student had learned.

And now the student didn't need the teacher anymore.

**

The second sign came three days later.

Bertoldo was crossing the great hall when he overheard two young courtiers—men barely twenty, new to the Duke's service, their doublets still stiff with newness.

"Did you hear?" one was saying. "Salviati is negotiating with Milan."

"Vittorio Salviati?" the other asked.

"The same. They say the Duke trusts him. Gave him the Milan embassy without consulting the old council."

"About time. The old guard has held this court too long. Salviati understands the future. He's from Milan himself—he knows their methods. Not like..." The young man lowered his voice, but not enough. "Not like the chamberlain. God knows Bertoldo was useful in his day, but his day was Federico's reign. This is Guidobaldo's court now."

They walked past Bertoldo without seeing him.

Of course they didn't see him. That was the point. That was what he'd spent thirty years perfecting—the ability to be in a room without being noticed, to witness without being witnessed.

But for the first time, invisibility felt less like power and more like erasure.

**

The third sign came at a feast celebrating Guidobaldo's name day.

The great hall blazed with light—candles in silver candelabra, torches in iron sconces, the massive fireplace roaring. The long tables groaned with roasted meats, fresh bread, early strawberries from the hothouse. Musicians played in the gallery. Nobles laughed. The air smelled of wine and success.

Bertoldo stood in his usual position—along the wall, where he could observe without participating. Where he could watch for problems before they escalated into crises.

The Duke rose to speak. The hall fell silent.

"Friends," Guidobaldo said, his voice carrying easily through the space. "This year has been prosperous. Our borders are secure. Our trade flourishes. Our people thrive. This success belongs to all of you."

He raised his cup, and the hall roared approval.

"I wish to thank those who have made this possible," Guidobaldo continued. "Lord Lorenzo Orsini, whose counsel has been invaluable. Lord Giacomo Rinaldi, whose merchant connections have filled our treasuries. Captain Monti, whose vigilance keeps our walls strong. And Vittorio Salviati, whose negotiations with Milan have opened new opportunities for our prosperity."

Each man stood as his name was called. Each received applause. Each basked in the Duke's recognition.

Bertoldo's name was not mentioned.

It shouldn't bother him. Recognition was antithetical to his work. The invisible servant survived longest. The chamberlain who craved glory became a target.

He knew this. He'd written it in his cipher journal twenty-seven years ago.

But knowledge and feeling were different countries.

A young noble—drunk, stumbling—backed into Bertoldo, nearly knocking him over. The noble turned, looked at him without recognition, mumbled something that might have been an apology, and staggered away.

The man hadn't seen him. Not really. Just a figure in dark wool. A servant. Invisible.

Bertoldo watched the Duke across the room. Guidobaldo was laughing with Vittorio Salviati—young, handsome, sharp-witted Salviati from Milan, who understood the new methods, the future methods.

And Bertoldo understood.

He'd succeeded.

He'd taught Guidobaldo so well that the Duke no longer needed him. He'd made himself indispensable for twenty-eight years, and now—precisely because he'd done his work correctly—he was dispensable.

The greatest achievement of a teacher is to become unnecessary.

He'd achieved it.

He left the feast early. No one noticed.

**

That night, he sat in his chambers and looked at the mirror—a small, precious thing, Italian glass, nearly clear. He'd owned it for fifteen years but rarely looked at it.

The face that looked back was old.

When had that happened? When had the lines deepened around his eyes? When had his hair gone completely gray? When had his hands developed those brown spots, those prominent veins?

He was fifty-one years old. He felt seventy.

He went to his desk and opened his cipher journal to a fresh page.

*1490. The Recognition.*

*I have seen the sign. Three signs, actually, but they are all the same sign: I am no longer necessary.*

*Guidobaldo made the salt tax decision without me. The courtiers speak of Salviati as the future. The Duke's toast did not include my name.*

*Each sign alone could be dismissed. Together, they form a verdict.*

*The gatekeepers obsolescence is the proof of his success. I taught the Duke to rule alone, and now he does. I made myself indispensable until I taught him how to be independent, and now I am neither.*

*The window is narrowing. I have perhaps six months—perhaps a year—before the polite suggestions begin. Before the honorary title is offered. Before the duties are quietly reassigned to younger men who understand the future.*

*I can wait for that humiliation, or I can leave while departure is still a choice.*

He stopped writing. His hand was shaking.

He went to the window and looked toward the south, toward the hills that hid Fano beyond the darkness. Somewhere out there was his father's villa. The olive trees he'd planted. The vineyard. The view of the valley.

A life that had been waiting for him for thirty years.

A life he'd told himself he would return to.

Tomorrow, he decided. Tomorrow he would begin planning his departure.

But even as he thought it, he knew the truth.

He would stay until he was pushed. Until the choice was taken from him. Until the humiliation arrived.

Because the chains he'd forged over three decades were too heavy to break.

Because he didn't know how to be anything except the man at the door.

Because Urbino needed a gatekeeper, and he had become the gate itself.

He closed the journal and went to bed.

He did not sleep.

II. The Ghost of Ferrara

That night, Bertoldo did not sleep. He paced his chambers, the stone floor cold beneath his feet, haunted not by the Duke's independence but by a memory from his youth.

He remembered Ludovico Renzo.

Renzo had been the chamberlain of Ferrara–a legend in his time, a man who had served four dukes. Bertoldo had met him once, twenty years ago, on a diplomatic mission. Renzo was eighty then. He was deaf in one ear. He fell asleep during negotiations. He clung to his position with arthritic, claw-like hands, terrified that if he let go, he would cease to exist.

The court mocked him. They whispered behind his back, countermanded his orders, treated him like a piece of furniture that had become too heavy to move but too historic to burn.

*The old bear who cannot smell the winter,* they called him.

Bertoldo stopped pacing. He looked at his hands–veined, spotted with age, but still steady. He looked at his reflection in the darkened window.

"I will not be him," he whispered to the empty room.

The temptation to stay was a physical thing, a hunger. To stay meant relevance. To stay meant power. To stay meant that when he walked down a corridor, men straightened; when he spoke, men wrote it down.

To leave was to become a ghost before one was dead.

But the alternative was to become a joke.

He sat at his desk and unlocked the iron chest. He opened the third volume of the cipher.

*From the cipher of Bertoldo di Fano:*

*The tragedy of the gatekeeper is not that he is replaced; it is that he is replaced because he succeeded. I built a prince who can rule without me. My obsolescence is the proof of my victory.*

*I must drink this victory, though it tastes like ash.*

*"I will not wait to be pushed. I will not wait for the polite suggestion, the diminished duties, the honorary title that signals the end. I will choose the hour. I will define the narrative. I will walk through the gate on my own feet, while I still hold the keys."*

III. The Architecture of Exit

The resignation of a man who holds the secrets of the state is not a simple matter of handing in a badge. It is a campaign. It requires as much strategy as a war, and more delicacy.

Bertoldo began the next morning.

He did not announce his departure. Instead, he began to make himself invisible in a new way. Where before he had hidden his hand to let the Duke take credit, now he hid his hand to let Sandro take the weight.

When the Florentine ambassador requested an audience, Bertoldo sent Sandro to conduct the preliminary interview. "My eyes are tired today," he lied. "You see him. Tell me if he is lying."

When the correspondence arrived from Rome, Bertoldo had Sandro break the seals. "Your fingers are younger. Read it to me."

He watched Sandro work, correcting him less and less. He saw the younger man's confidence grow, saw the court begin to turn toward the new sun. It was a slow, painful surgery–cutting himself out of the organism he had nurtured for thirty years–but he performed it without flinching.

By winter, the transfer was unseen but substantial. Sandro held the threads; Bertoldo was left with the title alone.

IV. The Breaking of Chains

Bertoldo requested the audience on a Monday morning in June. He sent a formal note—not a whispered word in passing, but a written request, sealed and delivered by a page. The formality was deliberate. It signaled that what followed was official.

Guidobaldo received him in the small solar, not the grand study. A private room. The summer sun slanted through the windows, warming the stone floor. The Duke sat not at his desk but in a chair by the window. He gestured to another chair.

"Sit," Guidobaldo said.

Bertoldo sat.

For a moment, neither spoke. The Duke looked at him with an expression Bertoldo couldn't quite read. Not cold. Not warm. Something between.

"You wish to resign," Guidobaldo said finally.

"How did you—" Bertoldo stopped. Of course he knew. The formality of the request had announced it.

"Why?" Guidobaldo asked.

"I'm old, Your Excellence. Tired. I've served thirty years. The time has come—"

"Don't lie to me." The Duke's voice was quiet but firm. "Not now. Not after everything."

Bertoldo closed his eyes briefly. Then he opened them and told the truth.

"Because you don't need me anymore."

Silence.

"The salt tax decision," Bertoldo continued. "You made it without consulting me. You were correct. It was exactly the choice I would have recommended. Which means I taught you well. Which means my work is done."

"So you're leaving because you succeeded?" Guidobaldo's voice carried an edge. "That's your reason?"

"I'm leaving before I become an obstacle."

"An obstacle to what?"

"To you." Bertoldo met the Duke's eyes. "Your Excellence, you're nineteen years old. You have fifty years of rule ahead of you, God willing. You need to build a court that serves you—your generation, your methods, your vision. Not your father's. Not mine."

"You served my father. You've served me. You could continue—"

"No." Bertoldo's voice was firm. "I can't. Because every decision you make while I'm here, men will wonder—did the Duke choose this, or did his chamberlain? Every success will be credited to my influence. Every failure will be blamed on my interference. As long as I remain, you rule in my shadow."

"I don't see it that way."

"The courtiers do. The ambassadors do. The merchants do." Bertoldo leaned forward. "And in time, Your Excellence, you will too. You'll make a decision you're confident in, and you'll see doubt in other men's eyes. You'll wonder if they doubt you or if they doubt that it was truly your choice. That doubt will poison you."

Guidobaldo stood abruptly, walked to the window. His hands gripped the stone sill.

"You're abandoning me."

The words were raw. Young. The voice not of a duke but of a boy who'd lost his father at thirteen and was about to lose the only other father figure he'd known.

Bertoldo felt something crack inside him.

"I'm setting you free," he said quietly.

"Free?" Guidobaldo whirled. "You think I feel trapped by your service? You think I want you gone?"

"Yes."

The word hung between them.

"Not consciously," Bertoldo added. "Not in a way you'd admit to yourself. But yes. Part of you wants this. Part of you has been waiting for me to leave. Part of you knows that until I do, you're not truly ruling."

Guidobaldo's face was pale. "You presume to know my mind?"

"I've watched you for seven years. I've taught you how to read other men. Do you think I can't read you?" Bertoldo stood. "Two months ago, at the salt tax council, you didn't look at me. You used to look at me before every decision. You used to wait for my nod. This time, you didn't. You solved it yourself."

"Because you taught me to!"

"Exactly!" Bertoldo's voice rose for the first time. "I taught you to rule without me. That was my job. And now my job is done."

They stared at each other across the sun-warmed room.

"What if I order you to stay?" Guidobaldo said quietly.

"Then I will obey."

"Then I order—"

"But if you do," Bertoldo interrupted, "you prove you're not ready to rule alone. You prove that you still need me. And we'll both know it. And that knowledge will corrode everything between us until you hate me for staying and hate yourself for needing me."

His voice dropped to barely above a whisper.

"And I'll stay, Your Excellence. I'll obey your command. I'll serve you until I die. But we'll both know that I should have left, and every day I remain will be a reminder that you couldn't let me go."

Guidobaldo's hands were shaking. "What if I fail without you?"

"Then you fail as a duke." Bertoldo's voice was gentle now. "Not as my puppet. You fail making your own choices, learning from your own mistakes, growing into your own authority. That's the only way to truly rule."

"I don't want you to go."

"I know."

"I need you."

"You did. You don't anymore. And in another year, you won't even remember why you thought you did."

Tears were running down Guidobaldo's face now. The young Duke made no move to wipe them away.

"Who will advise me?" he asked.

"Your council. Your captains. Vittorio Salviati—he's sharp, and his Milan connections will serve you well."

"Salviati isn't you."

"No. He's better. He's your age. He understands the world you're inheriting, not the world I was trained in. He speaks the language of the next thirty years, not the last thirty."

Bertoldo crossed to the Duke, stood before him. He wanted to embrace him but couldn't. The gulf of rank was still there. Would always be there.

"When do you want to go?" Guidobaldo asked finally.

"Summer. Give me three months to transfer my duties. I'll train Salviati—"

"Not Salviati." Guidobaldo wiped his eyes. "Sandro Torelli. He's who you've been preparing. Let him be chamberlain."

Bertoldo felt a surge of pride mixed with sorrow. The Duke had been watching. Had understood what Bertoldo was doing with Sandro. Had seen further than Bertoldo gave him credit for.

"Sandro will serve you well," Bertoldo said.

"Will you visit?" Guidobaldo asked. "From Fano? Will you come back to court occasionally? For feast days? For counsel?"

Bertoldo shook his head slowly. "No, Your Excellence. Clean breaks heal better than ones that are picked at. If I return for visits, men will still look to me. Still ask my opinion. Still undermine your authority by seeking mine. I must leave completely."

"So this is..." Guidobaldo's voice broke. "This is goodbye."

"Yes."

The Duke stood there, trying to master his emotions, trying to be what a duke should be. But he was nineteen, and he was losing the only father he'd had left.

He crossed the space between them and embraced Bertoldo.

It was brief. Awkward. The embrace of a young man who didn't quite know how to show affection. But Bertoldo felt the strength in those arms, the solid weight of the boy who'd become a man.

When they separated, Guidobaldo's face was composed again. The mask back in place.

"Thank you," the Duke said. "For everything. For my father. For me. For Urbino."

"You're welcome, Your Excellence."

"Will you..." Guidobaldo hesitated. "Will you be happy? In Fano?"

Bertoldo thought about the question. The villa. The olive trees. The silence.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "But I'll find out."

He bowed—deeply, formally, one last time.

Then he turned and left before the Duke could see his own tears start.

V. The Departure

The months passed with a terrifying speed.

Bertoldo moved through the palace like a man attending his own funeral. He saw the rooms he would never enter again, the people he would never manage again. He felt the cold ache of the amputation.

On the last day of August, 1490, the transfer was complete.

There was no ceremony. A ceremony would have been an admission that power was changing hands, and the official fiction was that power resided only in the Duke.

Bertoldo packed his few personal possessions–his clothes, a few books of philosophy, the cipher volumes locked in their iron chest. He left the seals of office on the desk where he had found them thirty years before.

Sandro walked him to the gate.

They did not speak. There was nothing left to say. The training was done. The secrets were shared. The burden had been transferred.

At the heavy iron gates, the guards snapped to attention. They saluted Bertoldo, but their eyes slid past him to Sandro–the new power, the man to watch.

It had already happened.

Bertoldo stepped out into the sunlight. The air was warm. The road to Fano stretched out before him, dusty and quiet.

He turned once to look back at the Palazzo Ducale. It rose against the sky, a fortress of brick and stone, a machine for turning human ambition into order. He knew every secret in its walls. He knew where the bodies were buried, literally and metaphorically. He knew the price of every stone.

He felt a sudden, crushing weight lift from his shoulders–a weight he hadn't realized he was carrying until it was gone.

 *Absence is not nothingness. It is an office vacated, a room that remembers who once stood in it.* 

"Guard it well," he said to Sandro.

"I will."

"And Sandro?"

"Yes?"

"Don't stay too long."

Bertoldo turned and walked away. He did not look back again.


–

VI. The Phantom Limb

The first month in Fano was paradise.

Bertoldo woke with the sun—not because duty demanded it, but because the light through his window was too beautiful to sleep through. He tended his olive trees, pruning with hands that slowly remembered the work they'd known as a boy. He read Seneca in the garden. He ate when he was hungry, not when the court schedule allowed.

No petitioners waited for him. No crises demanded his attention. No secrets gnawed at his conscience.

It was exactly the life he'd imagined for thirty years.

And for thirty days, it was enough.

Then the messenger arrived.

It was a Thursday in September. Bertoldo was in the vineyard, examining the grapes—they would be ready for harvest in another week. He heard hoofbeats on the road and looked up to see a rider in ducal livery dismounting at his gate.

His heart clenched. *Something's happened. Guidobaldo is hurt. The duchy is—*

But the rider's face was calm as he approached. "Signore Bertoldo? I have letters for you. From Urbino."

Not urgent, then. Just correspondence.

Bertoldo took the packet—three letters, sealed with the ducal insignia. He paid the messenger, offered him water and bread, then went inside to read.

The first letter was from Guidobaldo. Brief, formal:

*Maestro Bertoldo, I hope this finds you well. Urbino prospers. The harvest looks promising. I thought you would wish to know. —G.*

Simple. Correct. The letter of a duke to a retired servant. Properly distant.

Bertoldo set it aside with a strange ache in his chest.

The second letter was from Sandro. Longer:

*Maestro, A situation has developed with the Venetian wool contract. The ambassador is demanding terms that would give Venice preferential rates in perpetuity. The council is divided. Lorenzo Orsini argues for acceptance—the immediate revenue would fund the new fortifications. Giacomo Rinaldi argues for refusal—the long-term cost would cripple our independence. The Duke asked my opinion. I told him I needed to examine the historical precedents before advising. I've been in the archives for three days. I found the Doge's correspondence from 1467—the language they're using now mirrors what you rejected then. I believe I understand the pattern: they offer generous terms for five years, then quietly revise at renewal when we're dependent on their coin...*

The letter continued for two pages. Detailed analysis. Strategic thinking. Exactly the kind of work Bertoldo had trained him to do.

And Bertoldo found himself reading it not with pride but with *urgency*.

His mind immediately began solving the problem: *The Rinaldi solution is correct, but Lorenzo's concern about fortification funding is valid. The answer is a three-year contract, not perpetual, with language requiring re-negotiation of terms rather than automatic renewal. Pair it with a loan from Florence to fund the fortifications, using the Venetian contract as collateral...*

He was halfway through drafting a response before he realized what he was doing.

He set down the pen.

Looked at the half-written letter.

This wasn't his problem anymore.

He picked up the pen again. Crossed out what he'd written. Started over:

*Sandro, You've analyzed it correctly. Trust your judgment. Whatever you recommend, Guidobaldo will consider seriously. You don't need my confirmation. —B.*

He sealed it quickly, before he could add more.

The third letter was from a merchant in Urbino—someone Bertoldo had worked with years ago. Trade news. Market gossip. Nothing urgent.

Bertoldo set all three letters aside and returned to his vineyard.

But he couldn't focus on the grapes.

His mind kept returning to Sandro's letter. The Venetian contract. The council debate. What was Guidobaldo thinking? Had he considered the Florence option? Did Sandro know about the 1472 precedent, not just 1467?

By evening, he'd worn a path in the grass from pacing.

That night, he barely slept. He kept imagining the council meeting. Orsini arguing. Rinaldi countering. The Duke looking to Sandro for guidance. What if Sandro gave the wrong advice? What if the contract was signed and ten years from now Urbino realized its mistake?

At dawn, he found himself in his study, drafting a longer letter to Sandro. Detailed recommendations. Historical examples. Tactical suggestions.

He was three pages in when his neighbor knocked.

"Signore Bertoldo?" Old Paolo, who owned the neighboring farm. "I heard you walking last night. Your floorboards creak. Are you well?"

"I'm fine."

Paolo studied him. "You were talking to yourself. I could hear through the walls."

Bertoldo blinked. "I was?"

"Working through some problem. Something about Venice and fortifications." Paolo's weathered face showed concern. "Are you sure you're well? Should I fetch the physician?"

Bertoldo looked down at his hands. They were trembling. The three pages of advice lay on his desk—covered in his handwriting, dense with calculations and strategic alternatives.

He'd been working the problem in his sleep. Pacing his floors. Talking to himself.

"I'm..." He stopped. "I don't know."

Paolo stepped into the study. Looked at the letters, the draft response, the anxious energy radiating from Bertoldo.

"Come," the farmer said. "Work with me today. Harvest work. It'll keep your hands busy."

So Bertoldo spent the day in Paolo's fields, cutting wheat. Physical labor. Mindless, repetitive, exhausting.

But every break, every pause for water, his mind returned to Urbino. To the Venetian contract. To the duke who no longer needed him.

By evening, his back ached and his hands were blistered. But the ghost of the work still throbbed.

That night, he did something he'd resisted for thirty years: he poured himself a large cup of wine and drank until the questions stopped.

It didn't help.

**

A week later, another letter from Sandro:

*Maestro, Thank you for your advice to trust my judgment. I presented my recommendation—a three-year contract with re-negotiation language. The Duke accepted it. The Venetians grumbled but signed. Lorenzo Orsini has secured a loan from Florence for the fortifications. The situation is resolved. Your method worked, even without you here to guide it. —S.*

Bertoldo should have felt pride. Relief. Satisfaction that his student had succeeded.

Instead, he felt hollowed out.

The duchy had faced a crisis and solved it. Without him.

Life continued. The gate still stood. The realm still functioned.

And Bertoldo was...

*Unnecessary.*

He'd known it intellectually. But the ledger of the mind does not govern the ledger of the blood.

He went to his olive grove and worked until his hands bled. He tended his vines with savage intensity. He read philosophy until the words blurred.

But every messenger who passed his gate made his heart race. Every letter from Urbino made his mind begin calculating problems and solutions.

The phantom limb wouldn't stop aching.

One night, a month into his retirement, he woke from a dream in which he was back at his desk in the palazzo. Petitioners waited. Crises loomed. The Duke needed him.

He woke reaching for documents that weren't there.

He sat on the edge of his bed, head in his hands, and finally admitted the truth:

He didn't know how to be anything except the gatekeeper.

Thirty years of service hadn't just shaped his days. It had shaped his soul. Without the gate to guard, he was...

Empty.

He went to his study, lit a candle, opened his cipher journal.

*1490. The Third Month. Fano.*

*Retirement is not peace. It is withdrawal. The addiction to purpose does not fade simply because the purpose is taken away.*

*I tend trees I don't recognize. I read philosophy without applying it. I watch merchants pass and wonder what news they carry, what intelligence could be gleaned, what leverage exists in their trade routes.*

*I am not living in Fano. I am haunting it. A ghost of the man I was, unable to move on because I never learned how to be anything else.*

*The chain is broken. But the weight remains.*

*I don't know how to carry it. I don't know how to set it down.*

*I don't know—*

He stopped writing.

Through his window, he could see the road to Urbino, invisible in the darkness but present in his mind's geography. Two days' ride. Maybe three for an old man.

If he went back—just to visit, just to advise, just to help with one more crisis—it would be exactly the wrong thing to do.

But God, how he wanted to.

He closed the journal.

He did not go back.

Not that night.

Not yet.

But the phantom limb kept aching, and Bertoldo was beginning to understand that some chains don't break.

They just get longer.
